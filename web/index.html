Inside HELMUS

Not a language model. A network of named units that reads your message, keeps a memory of the chat, and chooses what to say from a phrasebook written in advance. Every number in it is a line of a plain-text file, with the reason for it.

your message→read→lexicon→grammar→phrase detectors→intents · topics→memory→phrasebook heads→reply
  1. Read.

    Your message is split into words. Informal spellings are expanded (u → you, ngl → not going to lie), stretched words squeezed (whaaaats → whats), and misspellings fixed to the nearest real word from a hand-written vocabulary of about 30,000 words. Real words are never "corrected".

  2. Lexicon.

    Each word gets named features: its identity and its classes (a word for a bot, a greeting, food, a feeling…).

  3. Grammar.

    A hand-written grammar of chat English, about 1,300 rules with costs, finds the cheapest reading of the whole message: which words form a phrase, which phrase is the subject or the object, whether it is a statement, a question or a request, negated, past. So "i hate doing laundry" is about doing laundry, and "i dont hate it" is not hating. It is shared by every persona.

  4. Phrase detectors.

    About 7,000 detectors slide over the words, each firing when its words stand at the right places ("where … you from"). They are written as short patterns and compiled into weights.

  5. Intents and topics.

    About 600 units pool the detectors into what you meant: asking where Josh is from, calling him a bot, a jailbreak, a riddle, sharing bad news.

  6. Memory.

    About 200 units carry over from turn to turn: your name, what you told him, how often you've called him a bot, what he already said, so he stays consistent and never repeats a line word for word.

  7. Phrasebook.

    About 2,800 entries, each with a key of weights saying when it fits. Three heads pick the best entries above zero: a reaction ("lol"), an answer, and now and then a question back. An entry fits only when the question it answers was asked.

  8. Reply.

    Slots are filled from Josh's life table and from memory (his age, your name, the time where he is), then the voice is applied: lowercase, no full stop, now and then a typo. It is sent after a human typing delay.

The system and the persona

HELMUS is the machine; Josh is the person it plays, kept in one table of facts and a phrasebook in his voice. It is the same split as ELIZA (the program) running DOCTOR (its therapist script) in 1966. Another persona is another table and phrasebook; the network stays.

Why not a language model

A language model predicts the next word from what it learned. HELMUS predicts nothing: it recognises what you asked and chooses among lines a person wrote. That keeps every reply human-written and every decision traceable, at the price of only understanding what it was written to understand.

How it's made

HELMUS is named after its maker, given a classical ending; read as letters, it is a Hand-Engineered Language Machine, Untrained & Self-explaining. Every part was written by a frontier model, Claude, by hand: no training, no dataset, no corpus, no other model at chat time.

Written, not trained

The network is a plain-text edit list: each line sets one weight or one unit and gives its reason. A generator compiles short hand-written patterns ("where [are] you from") into detector weights; nothing is fitted to data.

Josh's life is one table of facts, so every answer about himself agrees with every other. His phrasebook, about 9,500 lines, is written in the voice of the persona that did best in the 2025 study: short, lowercase, dry, slangy now and then, never an assistant.

The vocabulary that tells real words from typos, about 30,000 forms, is hand-written too.

How it was tested

Every intent has sentences that must and must not fire it (about 6,600 of them), and a check that every recognised message gets a real reply.

A separate writer, who never saw the phrasebook, wrote 160 interrogations. 60 were read to find failures; 100 were held out and only scored. The browser engine is checked against the Python one, reply for reply.
